Electrolyte Imbalances

Na, K, Ca, Mg and phos: normals, ECG clues, safe replacement, hyperkalemia steps

Checked against current sources October 2026

Normals and key signs

Exam study summary, not a bedside tool. Doses are typical adult ranges; follow your institution's protocols.

Exam tip Low Mg keeps K and Ca low: if K will not correct, check and replace Mg.

Hyperkalemia: stabilize, shift, remove

  1. Stabilize (ECG changes): calcium gluconate 10% 30 mL over 10 min; in arrest or peri-arrest, calcium chloride 10% 10 mL over 5 min (same calcium). Acts in about 3 min, lasts 30–60 min; repeat if changes persist.
  2. Shift: regular insulin 10 units IV with dextrose 25–50 g. K falls within 15 min, lowest at 30–60 min.
  3. Add-on shift: nebulized albuterol 10–20 mg, never alone. Bicarbonate mainly for metabolic acidosis.
  4. Remove: binders (sodium zirconium cyclosilicate acts in about 1 h, patiromer 4–7 h), loop diuretic, dialysis (definitive).
  5. Recheck K often; it rebounds as the shift wears off.
Exam tip Exams test the why: calcium protects the heart but does not lower K; insulin and albuterol shift K in; binders, diuretics and dialysis remove it.
Safety Insulin-glucose can cause hypoglycemia, usually 1–3 h later; check glucose often for 6 h. High K with a normal ECG may be hemolysis, but treat ECG changes now.

In cardiac arrest (AHA 2025), calcium, bicarbonate and insulin-glucose may be considered but are unproven; inhaled albuterol is not recommended.

Potassium and magnesium replacement

  • Check urine output and kidney function first.
  • IV KCl: always diluted, always on a pump.
  • Peripheral IV: usually no faster than 10 mEq/h.
  • Central line, continuous ECG: often up to 20 mEq/h per policy; the label allows 40 only if K is under 2 with ECG changes or paralysis.
  • DKA: if K is below 3.5, give K first and hold insulin (ADA 2024).
  • Torsades (polymorphic VT with long QT): magnesium sulfate 1–2 g IV; defibrillate (unsynchronized) if pulseless or sustained. Mg is not advised for normal-QT polymorphic VT.
  • Mg toxicity (preeclampsia drip): reflexes fade, then breathing slows. Stop the Mg; give IV calcium gluconate.
Safety Never give KCl IV push. Concentrated KCl can stop the heart.

Calcium: salts and corrected calcium

FeatureGluconate 10%Chloride 10%
Elemental Ca per 10 mLAbout 93 mgAbout 270 mg (3 times more)
LinePeripheral OK (secure IV)Central or deep vein; necrosis if it leaks

Corrected Ca = Ca + 0.8 × (4.0 − albumin g/dL). Example: Ca 7.6, albumin 2.0 → 9.2 mg/dL, not truly low.

Exam tip Low albumin lowers total Ca, not ionized Ca. Exams give the albumin to see if you correct first.

Low ionized Ca: alkalosis, massive transfusion (citrate), thyroid or parathyroid surgery, pancreatitis.

Safety In the ICU, measure ionized Ca; the formula often misses true hypocalcemia. Never mix calcium with bicarbonate, phosphate or ceftriaxone. Caution with digoxin.

High Ca: IV saline first. If severe (over 14 mg/dL), calcitonin (fast, fades in 48–72 h) plus an IV bisphosphonate or denosumab (slower, longer acting).

Sodium: correct slowly

  • Severe symptoms (seizures, coma): 3% saline 100–150 mL over 10–20 min, repeated to raise Na about 5 mEq/L.
  • Chronic: raise Na no more than 8 mEq/L in any 24 h; guideline upper limits run 10–12.
  • ODS risk: very low Na, alcohol use, malnutrition, liver disease, low K.
  • ODS shows days later: dysarthria, dysphagia, paralysis.
  • Overcorrected: stop saline; re-lower with D5W, desmopressin on expert advice.
  • Hypernatremia: give free water; lower chronic cases slowly, over about 48 h.
Safety Too fast is the danger: raising low Na risks osmotic demyelination; dropping chronic high Na risks cerebral edema.

Causes, refeeding, phosphate

  • High K: AKI or CKD, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, spironolactone, rhabdo or burns, acidosis, transfusion.
  • Tumor lysis: high K, phos and uric acid; low Ca.
  • Refeeding: phos, K and Mg fall within 5 days of restarting nutrition (ASPEN 2020).
  • IV phosphate: slowly over hours, never with calcium in the same line.
