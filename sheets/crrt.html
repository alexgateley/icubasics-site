CRRT (Continuous Renal Replacement)

Modes, dose, circuit pressures, citrate and nursing care for critical care exams

Checked against current sources October 2026

Indications: why continuous

CRRT removes fluid and solutes slowly, around the clock, so shifts are small. It suits unstable ICU patients with AKI.

  • A: severe metabolic acidosis, refractory to treatment
  • E: refractory hyperkalemia
  • I: dialyzable toxins (lithium, methanol); intermittent HD preferred
  • O: fluid overload or pulmonary edema despite diuretics
  • U: uremic pericarditis, encephalopathy or bleeding

KDIGO suggests CRRT over intermittent HD for hemodynamic instability and for brain injury or high ICP. EASL advises CRRT in acute liver failure, including for persistent high ammonia.

Modalities and dose

All modes can also remove fluid (net ultrafiltration).
ModeMechanismFluid usedClears
SCUFUltrafiltrationNoneFluid only
CVVHConvectionReplacementSmall + middle molecules
CVVHDDiffusionDialysateSmall solutes (K, urea)
CVVHDFBothBothSmall + middle molecules
Exam tip Diffusion down a dialysate gradient best clears small solutes (K, urea). Convection is solvent drag from high-volume ultrafiltration, driven by TMP; it also pulls middle molecules. Replacement fluid gives back the volume removed. SCUF removes water, almost no solute.
  • Dose = effluent rate (dialysate + replacement + fluid removed)
  • KDIGO: deliver 20–25 mL/kg/h
  • Prescribe about 25–30 mL/kg/h to allow for downtime and pre-dilution
  • Example: 80 kg × 25 = 2,000 mL/h
  • 35–40 mL/kg/h (ATN, RENAL trials) added no survival benefit
  • Bicarbonate-buffered fluids, not lactate, especially in shock or liver failure

Typical adult targets for exam study. Follow your institution's protocols.

Access and circuit pressures

  • Nontunneled dialysis catheter, placed with ultrasound
  • KDIGO site order: right IJ, femoral, left IJ, subclavian last
  • Subclavian: central vein stenosis. Femoral: recirculation, immobility, infection risk in obesity
  • Chest x-ray before first use of an IJ or subclavian line
  • Dialysis catheter for RRT only, except in emergencies

Access (draw)NegativeMore negative: kink, clot, wall contact, low volume
Filter (pre-filter)Positive, highestRising: clotting or return-side block

Safety A sudden fall in return pressure can mean disconnection: rapid blood loss, and air entry through an open lumen. Keep connections visible and secured.
Exam tip Exams link rising TMP and pressure drop to a clotting filter, and very negative access pressure to a catheter problem. Check kinks, clamps and position first.

Anticoagulation

KDIGO suggests regional citrate over heparin when citrate is safe: longer filter life, less bleeding.

FeatureCitrate (regional)Heparin (systemic)
ActionBinds Ca in circuit; Ca replaced; liver makes HCO3Boosts antithrombin
MonitorPost-filter iCa 0.25–0.35; patient iCa 1.1–1.3 mmol/LaPTT or anti-Xa, platelets
RisksLow Ca, alkalosis, accumulationBleeding, HIT
Avoid / cautionSevere liver failure, shock with rising lactate (accumulation)Active bleeding; never in HIT

Low iCa signs: tingling lips or fingertips, cramps, tetany, long QT, seizures.

Safety Citrate accumulation: patient iCa falls despite more calcium, total Ca rises, the total-to-ionized ratio is 2.5 or more (same units), and an anion gap acidosis appears. Report it; protocols cut or stop citrate.

Metabolic alkalosis usually means citrate overload (citrate becomes bicarbonate). In HIT, stop all heparin, including catheter locks, flushes and priming; use a non-heparin anticoagulant such as argatroban (liver-cleared, aPTT-monitored) per protocol.

Filter clotting

  • Signs: rising TMP and pressure drop, dark streaks in the filter, falling clearance
  • Causes: catheter flow problems (most common), weak anticoagulation, post-dilution filtration fraction above about 20–25%
  • Filters clotting early again and again: suspect HIT
  • Prevention: pre-dilution, CVVHD or CVVHDF over CVVH, good catheter position
  • When pressures show clotting, return the blood promptly per protocol; a clotted circuit's blood is lost

Nursing care: fluid, labs, drugs

  • Hourly balance: machine removal plus all patient intake and output, against a daily goal
  • Net removal above about 1.75 mL/kg/h was linked to higher mortality (observational)
  • BMP, Mg, phosphate every 6–12 h at start, then 12–24 h; on citrate, iCa more often per protocol
  • Low K, Mg and phosphate are common; low phosphate can delay vent weaning
  • Hypothermia: the circuit loses heat; use the warmer; it can mask fever
  • Drugs: water-soluble, low-protein-bound drugs (vancomycin, beta-lactams) are removed; underdosing is the risk
  • Drug levels and pharmacist review; reassess doses when CRRT stops or changes
