IABP Timing

Counterpulsation, triggers, the four timing errors, nursing care and weaning

Checked against current sources October 2026

Download one-page PDFLetter landscape, ready to print or keep with your notes

How counterpulsation works

A balloon in the descending thoracic aorta fills with helium in diastole and empties just before systole. Light helium moves in and out fast. Support is modest: about 0.5 to 1 L/min of added cardiac output.

PhaseLandmarkEffect
InflateMid T wave; dicrotic notchRaises diastolic pressure and coronary perfusion
DeflateR wave; just before the upstrokeLowers end-diastolic pressure, afterload and O2 demand

Position: tip about 2 cm below the left subclavian (on x-ray, about 2 cm above the carina), lower end above the renal arteries. Check on daily x-ray.

Signs it is working: augmentation above the patient's systole, lower assisted end-diastolic and systolic pressures, rising MAP, cardiac index and urine output, falling PAWP, less chest pain.

Indications and contraindications

Short-term bridge in:

  • Cardiogenic shock with a mechanical complication (acute MR, ventricular septal rupture)
  • Refractory ischemia awaiting revascularization
  • High-risk PCI
  • Failure to wean from cardiopulmonary bypass
  • Refractory ventricular arrhythmias; bridge to VAD or transplant

Contraindications:

  • Moderate to severe aortic regurgitation
  • Aortic dissection or aneurysm
  • Severe peripheral arterial disease
  • Relative: bleeding disorder, uncontrolled infection
Exam tip Classic exam answer: aortic insufficiency is the key contraindication. Diastolic inflation drives blood back through the leaky valve and overloads the LV.
Exam tip Guideline shift: IABP-SHOCK II showed no 30-day survival benefit in MI-related shock (39.7% vs 41.3% mortality). ACC/AHA (2025) and ESC advise against routine IABP there unless a mechanical complication exists; selected STEMI shock may get a microaxial flow pump instead. Older items may still call shock the classic indication.

  • Check the tubing for blood all shift; in one cohort the gas-loss alarm caught only 29% of ruptures
  • Head of bed no higher than 30° so the catheter does not kink
  • Anticoagulation per protocol; heparin use varies
  • Balloon still for over 30 minutes risks clot. If the pump fails and no leak is suspected, inflate and deflate by hand per manufacturer instructions
Safety Suspected perforation: pump to standby, disconnect the extender tubing so the balloon deflates, clamp the catheter tubing, notify the provider and prepare for prompt removal. Never keep pumping or hand-inflate a leaking balloon: gas embolism or a trapped, clotted balloon can result.

Weaning

  • Start when stable on little vasoactive support (one proposed set: MAP >65, HR <100, CI >2.2, lactate <2)
  • Step down by assist ratio (1:1, 1:2, 1:3) or by balloon volume, per protocol; neither has better outcomes
  • Reassess BP, HR, CI, PAWP, urine output and lactate after each step
  • After removal: hold pressure, then check the site and distal pulses

Counterpulsation physiology in numbers

The balloon displaces its own volume, 25–50 mL, twice every cycle. Inflation at aortic valve closure pushes that volume toward the coronary ostia and arch vessels and down toward the viscera, so peak diastolic pressure rises above the patient's own systolic; deflation just before the valve opens removes it again, so the ventricle ejects into a lower pressure.

Typical numbers with correct timing (Gillespie 2024): assisted end-diastolic pressure 15–20 mm Hg below unassisted (older nursing texts and this app's question bank say 5–15), assisted systolic about 5 mm Hg lower (Parissis 2016 allows up to 10%), cardiac output up 0.5–1 L/min, PAWP and LVEDP down, mean pressure up. Demand falls with afterload and wall stress; supply rises with coronary perfusion pressure (aortic diastolic minus LVEDP). The pump adds no flow of its own, so a ventricle that barely ejects gains little, which is the physiological reading of IABP-SHOCK II; and Kern 1993 found coronary flow beyond a critical stenosis did not rise until the lesion was opened, so the pump buys time for revascularization rather than replacing it.

Exam tip Exam framing: inflation is supply (diastolic pressure, coronary perfusion), deflation is demand (afterload, end-diastolic pressure). Every timing error is graded by which of the two it spoils.

Catheters, balloons and consoles

Adult catheters are 7–8 Fr: Getinge Linear 7.5 Fr, Sensation 7 Fr and Sensation Plus 7.5–8 Fr (fiber-optic), and Teleflex Arrow UltraFlex 7.5 Fr and FiberOptix 8 Fr. The outer lumen carries helium; the inner takes the guidewire and then transmits aortic pressure. A fiber-optic tip needs no flush; a transduced inner lumen needs a 3 mL/h flush and is never used for blood sampling (Getinge). Membranes run 22–27.5 cm long and 14–18 mm wide; the inflated balloon should fill no more than 90% of the aortic diameter (Gillespie 2024; Teleflex teaches 90–95%).

Who gets one and who should not

The pump helps when the problem is ischemia or afterload and the ventricle still ejects; it cannot oxygenate, unload the right heart or rescue a ventricle with no stroke volume. Among 5,495 MI patients in the Benchmark Registry (Stone 2003), shock and support during PCI each made up 27% of indications and mechanical complications 12%; guidelines have since moved shock without a mechanical lesion off the list.

  • Mechanical complications of MI (papillary muscle or septal rupture): afterload reduction cuts the regurgitant or shunt fraction until surgery; ESC 2023 ACS IIa
  • High-risk PCI with LVEF 30% or less: BCIS-1 found fewer procedural complications (1.3% vs 10.7%) but no fewer in-hospital events, so use is selective
  • Failure to wean from bypass; bridge to decision, VAD or transplant, sometimes via the axillary artery so the patient can walk
  • MI shock without a mechanical lesion: not routine (ACC/AHA 2025 Class 3 No Benefit; ESC 2023 III). Heart-failure shock: no benefit in ALTSHOCK-2

Contraindications follow the same physiology. Moderate or severe aortic regurgitation: each inflation drives blood back through the valve and raises LVEDP; mild AR is relative. Aortic dissection, aneurysm or a fresh graft: the balloon can extend the tear. Severe aorto-iliac disease or small vessels: limb ischemia, for which the Benchmark Registry (Ferguson 2001) named female sex, peripheral arterial disease, body surface area under 1.65 m² and age 75 or older as predictors. Uncontrolled sepsis, severe coagulopathy and no plan beyond the pump are relative.

Exam tip Older items call cardiogenic shock the classic indication. Answer the item's framing, and know the current one: routine IABP in MI shock has no benefit; shock with a mechanical complication is still an indication.

Insertion, setup and reading the console

Insertion is Seldinger through the common femoral artery, usually under fluoroscopy. The operator measures from the puncture to the umbilicus and up to the sternal angle to estimate depth, advances the wrapped balloon over the wire and confirms the radiopaque tip 1–2 cm below the left subclavian (Gillespie 2024: 1–2 cm distal to the aortic knob), with a sheathed balloon fully clear of the sheath. In Auto or AutoPilot, START unwraps and fills the balloon, picks lead and trigger, sets timing and calibrates a fiber-optic sensor; the catheter is sutured, the centimeter mark at the skin recorded, and the augmentation alarm set about 3 minutes later.

Balloon pressure is the gas, not the patient: a rounded plateau means the balloon is squeezed (small aorta, sheath, flexed hip), a spike without a plateau means a kink or an unopened membrane.

  1. Set 1:2 and put the reference line on the dicrotic notch of an unassisted beat: inflation should align with it and make a sharp V. A notch visible before the rise is late inflation; a rise cutting into the systolic downslope is early
  2. Is augmentation higher than the patient's systolic? If not, look at rate, volume, filling, SVR and position before touching timing
  3. Does deflation lower assisted systolic and assisted end-diastolic? A U-shaped dip after the peak with no fall in the next systole is early deflation; an assisted end-diastolic at or above unassisted with a slow next upstroke is late
  4. Correct it: in Semi-Auto or Operator mode move the inflation or deflation point a few milliseconds and re-read; in Auto, fix the inputs (lead, gain, trigger, pressure source). Return to 1:1 and chart the six pressures
Safety Time from the console's aortic trace, never from a radial line, which arrives later with a lower diastolic. Recheck after any change in rate, rhythm or pressure source and after transport.

If the console stops and no leak is suspected, keep the balloon moving (Getinge): detach the extender, fit a 3-way stopcock and syringe to the male luer, aspirate to confirm no blood returns, inflate with 40 cc of air or helium and aspirate at once, every 5 minutes until a replacement arrives; never inject into the inner lumen. In cardiac arrest the pump stays on: pressure trigger if compressions produce a usable upstroke (Getinge and Gillespie 2024), otherwise internal at 80 per minute, then back to ECG when ejection returns; the console is defibrillator-proof. Restriction, unwrapping and console swaps go to the MCS team; removal is the physician's call.

Complications: prevention, recognition, management

The Benchmark Registry (Ferguson 2001, 16,909 patients) put major complications at 2.6%: major limb ischemia 0.9%, severe bleeding 0.8–0.9%, death caused by the device 0.05%. Parissis 2011 reports thrombocytopenia in up to half of patients and 1.5 times the complication rate once support passes 60 hours.

Perforation comes from calcified plaque abrading the membrane. The gas-loss alarm caught only 29% of ruptures in the series Sun 2026 cites, so Getinge asks for a tubing check throughout therapy, and newer Cardiosave software adds a Blood Suspected – Check Catheter and Tubing alarm. Blood in the lumen means helium can embolize through the tear and blood in the balloon clots within minutes: Standby, disconnect the extender so the balloon deflates, clamp the catheter tubing, notify, remove.

Safety Never flush or inject into the helium lumen, never hand-inflate a balloon suspected of leaking, never pull against resistance. Each turns a perforation into an embolism or an entrapment.

Daily care, anticoagulation and transport

  • Neuro: consciousness, pupils and limb movement every 1–2 hours; a new deficit is an embolus or dissection until proven otherwise
  • Vascular: both legs for pulses (Doppler if needed), color, temperature, sensation and movement every 15 minutes for the first hour, then hourly; left radial pulse hourly
  • Position: head of bed 30° or less (Gillespie 2024), cannulated leg straight, log roll; centimeter mark unchanged; chest film daily and after transport
  • Hematologic: platelets daily, aPTT or anti-Xa if heparin runs; inspect the gas tubing every hour
  • Device: helium, battery and AC icons, mode, trigger, ratio, volume and the six pressures charted hourly, timing checked at 1:2 every 4 hours; 60-mL syringe, 3-way stopcock and spare extender at the bedside

Anticoagulation varies more than any other part of IABP care. Many US units run unfractionated heparin to an aPTT of 50–70 seconds (Crimi 2025); others give none, especially after cardiac surgery. Pucher 2012 found one randomized trial (Jiang 2003, 153 patients): limb ischemia 4.2% with heparin vs 2.4% without, no thrombus in either arm, bleeding 14.1% vs 2.4%, and concluded that heparin should follow the patient's other indications, not the pump.

Transport (Gillespie 2024; Cardiosave transport guide): confirm position on film, record pulses, site and the six pressures, secure the catheter and extender, set Auto or AutoPilot with the console's own ECG leads so the trigger does not depend on the transport monitor, and check timing. Carry a full helium reservoir (36 Autofill cycles), two charged batteries or the transport power supply, an adapter for the receiving console and the manual-inflation syringe. Vibration corrupts the ECG, so a pressure trigger from the fiber-optic tip is the fallback. Helium expands as cabin pressure falls, so the Cardiosave purges and refills when ambient pressure drops 25 mm Hg (about every 1,000 ft of climb) or rises 50 mm Hg (every 2,000 ft of descent).

Weaning, removal and aftercare

Weaning starts when the reason for the pump has been treated (revascularization done, surgery over, rhythm controlled) and the patient is stable on little vasoactive support; one proposed set is MAP above 65 mm Hg, heart rate under 100, cardiac index over 2.2 L/min/m², lactate under 2 mmol/L and no new ischemia (Dangl 2023, written for temporary MCS in general). After each step recheck pressure, heart rate, cardiac index or ScvO₂, PAWP, urine output and lactate.

Low ratio and low volume are stations on the way out, not places to stay.
StrategyHowEvidence
Ratio weaning1:1 to 1:2 for hours, then 1:3 briefly, then outMost common (Rogers 2016); outcomes equal
Volume weaningAugmentation reduced stepwise toward minimumOne RCT favored it on hemodynamics (Rogers 2016)

Removal: hold heparin until the aPTT is near normal (many units wait 2–4 hours or until the ACT is below 150–180 seconds), put the console in Standby so the balloon is deflated and aspirated, withdraw the balloon into the sheath if one is in and remove both together, let the artery bleed back for a beat to flush clot, then hold pressure 30–45 minutes. Afterward: head of bed 30° or less with the leg straight for 4–6 hours, pulses and site every 15 minutes for an hour, then hourly; watch for retroperitoneal bleeding (flank pain, falling hemoglobin, tachycardia) and pseudoaneurysm (pulsatile mass, bruit).

Exam tip Exam framing for weaning: step down by ratio or volume per protocol, reassess after each step, and stop for angina, ectopy, falling MAP or cardiac index, or rising PAWP.

IABP-SHOCK II (Thiele 2012; 600 patients, nearly all revascularized early): mortality 39.7% vs 41.3% at 30 days (relative risk 0.96), 52% vs 51% at 12 months (Thiele 2013) and 66.3% vs 67.0% at 6 years (Thiele 2019), with the same complications (major bleeding 3.3% vs 4.4%, peripheral ischemia 4.3% vs 3.4%). BCIS-1 is the one trial with a late signal: mortality at 51 months 27.8% vs 38.7% (Perera 2013).

Guidelines followed. The 2004 ACC/AHA STEMI guideline had IABP in shock at Class I; ACC/AHA dropped it to IIa in 2013, ESC moved routine use to Class III while keeping IIa for mechanical complications (a split the 2023 ESC ACS guideline retains), and the 2025 ACC/AHA ACS guideline lists routine IABP, and routine VA-ECMO, as Class 3 No Benefit while giving a microaxial flow pump a 2a for selected STEMI shock on DanGer Shock (Møller 2024), whose survival gain came with severe bleeding, limb ischemia, hemolysis or device failure in 24.0% vs 6.2%.

Unsettled: whether any shock subgroup benefits (insertion before PCI, SCAI stage B–C, patients too frail for a larger device), whether heparin helps or only bleeds, which weaning method is better, and whether axillary IABP as an ambulatory bridge to transplant changes outcomes.

Exam pearls

  • Inflate at the dicrotic notch (mid T wave); deflate just before systole (on the R wave): the two landmarks exams ask most
  • Correct timing: augmented diastolic above unassisted systolic; assisted end-diastolic 15–20 below unassisted; assisted systolic about 5 lower
  • Early inflation and late deflation make the LV eject against an inflated balloon: higher afterload, LVEDP and oxygen demand
  • Early deflation wastes afterload reduction and may pull coronary flow backward (angina); late inflation shortens coronary perfusion time
  • Aortic regurgitation is the classic contraindication; aortic dissection and severe peripheral arterial disease are the others
  • Weak left radial pulse means the balloon is too high; falling urine output means it is too low
  • Blood or rust flecks in the gas tubing means perforation: Standby, disconnect, clamp, notify, remove; never hand-inflate a leaking balloon
  • ECG is the first-choice trigger, pressure during CPR, pacer when 100% paced, internal only without ejection
  • A still balloon clots: 30 minutes is the limit; hand-inflate every 5 minutes if the console fails
  • IABP-SHOCK II: 30-day mortality 39.7% vs 41.3%; routine IABP in MI shock is Class 3 (ACC/AHA 2025) and III (ESC 2023), with IIa for mechanical complications
