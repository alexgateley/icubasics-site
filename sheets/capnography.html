Capnography

Normal values, waveform patterns, CPR and ROSC, tube checks and sedation monitoring

Checked against current sources October 2026

Normal values and phases

EtCO₂: the CO₂ at the end of each exhaled breath. Typical adult values for exam study.

ItemNormal
EtCO₂35–45 mm Hg
PaCO₂ minus EtCO₂About 2–5 mm Hg
BaselineReturns to zero
ShapeRectangular: steep rise, plateau flat or gently rising
PhaseWhat it shows
IFlat baseline: dead-space gas, no CO₂
IISteep upstroke as alveolar gas arrives
IIIAlveolar plateau; EtCO₂ read at its end
DownstrokeInspiration: falls to zero (phase 0; some texts say IV)

A wider PaCO₂–EtCO₂ gap means more dead space: low cardiac output, hypovolemia, PE, emphysema. The gap varies in sick lungs, so match EtCO₂ to an ABG before trending it.

Patterns and causes

PatternThink
Sudden drop to zeroDisconnection, extubation or dislodged tube, complete obstruction, apnea
Sudden drop, not to zeroLeak, partial obstruction, abrupt fall in cardiac output (PE, arrest)
Sudden riseROSC, IV sodium bicarbonate, tourniquet release
Gradual riseHypoventilation, fever; malignant hyperthermia if it climbs despite more ventilation
Gradual fallHyperventilation, hypothermia, falling cardiac output
Shark finBronchospasm (asthma, COPD), kinked or plugged tube
Baseline above zeroRebreathing, exhausted CO₂ absorber, contaminated sensor
Notch in plateauCurare cleft: breathing effort as paralysis wears off
Safety When the trace suddenly flattens, assess the patient before the monitor: tube, circuit, breathing, pulse.
Exam tip Exams pair shark fin with bronchospasm, sudden zero with a disconnected or dislodged tube, and gradual rise with hypoventilation.

CPR quality and ROSC (AHA 2025)

During CPR, EtCO₂ mainly reflects the blood flow compressions generate. The 2025 algorithm: if EtCO₂ is low or falling, reassess CPR quality.

EtCO₂ during CPRSuggests
Under 10 mm HgLittle flow: check depth, rate, recoil, pauses; swap compressors
10 or more, ideally 20 or moreLinked to adequate compressions and more ROSC
Abrupt, sustained risePossible ROSC (a jump over 10 mm Hg is suggestive; smaller rises occur); confirm with pulse or arterial line
10 or less after 20 min of ALS, intubatedOne factor in deciding to stop, never the only one

Ventilation rate, epinephrine and sodium bicarbonate also shift EtCO₂. With an advanced airway, give 1 breath every 6 seconds.

Exam tip Low or falling EtCO₂: fix compressions, check the ventilation rate, confirm the tube. Abrupt sustained rise: check for ROSC. Older materials quote about 40 mm Hg; the 2025 guideline names no cutoff.
Safety In patients who are not intubated, no EtCO₂ cutoff should be used to stop resuscitation (AHA 2025, Class 3: Harm).

Confirming tube placement

  • In cardiac arrest, waveform capnography plus clinical assessment is the most reliable way to confirm and monitor the tube (AHA 2025, Class 1). ASA standards: verify every tracheal tube or supraglottic airway with exhaled CO₂.
  • Look for sustained CO₂: peaks that rise and fall with each breath and hold or grow over at least 7 breaths. CO₂ that fades suggests the esophagus.
  • In arrest, CO₂ can be low, but good compressions should not leave a completely flat trace.
  • Colorimetric detector: purple = no CO₂, yellow = CO₂; check the device's key. Arrest can give a false negative; gastric acid or drugs, a false positive that does not change breath to breath.
  • Keep monitoring; sudden loss may be the first sign of dislodgement.
Safety No trace = wrong place, even in arrest. Consensus guidance makes removing the tube the default; if it stays, recheck with video laryngoscopy, bronchoscopy or ultrasound. Breath sounds cannot rule out the esophagus.

Sedation and opioids

ASA standards (amended 2025): during moderate or deep sedation, observe ventilation continually and make every effort to monitor exhaled CO₂.

  • Capnography shows hypoventilation and apnea before SpO₂ falls, especially when oxygen keeps saturation up (procedural sedation, opioid PCA). It is linked to fewer desaturations.
  • Slow breathing (typical of opioids): high EtCO₂, tall, wide waveform.
  • Shallow breathing (typical of sedative-hypnotics): small waveform, normal or low number, while PaCO₂ rises. A low reading is not reassuring.
  • Shrinking or absent waveform while the chest moves: obstruction; open the airway.
  • Flat line, no effort: apnea; stimulate and ventilate.
  • Oxygen dilutes nasal cannula samples, so readings run low; compare trend and shape with baseline.
