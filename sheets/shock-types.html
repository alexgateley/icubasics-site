Shock Types at a Glance

Hemodynamic patterns, exam findings and first treatments for each shock type

Checked against current sources October 2026

Hemodynamic patterns

Shock: cells get or use too little oxygen; hypotension is not required. Hypovolemic, cardiogenic and obstructive shock are low-flow; distributive is low-resistance. Lactate >2 mmol/L marks hypoperfusion in any type; trend it to judge response.

Untreated patterns; mixed shock is common. Late or under-resuscitated sepsis can turn cold, with low CO and SvO2.

Findings and first treatments

TypeExam findingsFirst treatments
HypovolemicCool, pale, clammy; flat neck veins; narrow pulse pressureStop the loss; 1:1:1 blood products for bleeding; crystalloid otherwise
CardiogenicCool, mottled; crackles, JVD; low urine outputReperfuse an MI; norepinephrine; add an inotrope; MCS if failing
ObstructiveJVD plus muffled heart tones, absent breath sounds or sudden hypoxemiaRelieve it: chest decompression, pericardiocentesis, reperfusion for massive PE
SepticWarm, flushed early; fever; cold lateAntibiotics within 1 h; ≥30 mL/kg balanced crystalloid in 3 h; norepinephrine
AnaphylacticHives, angioedema, wheeze or stridorIM epinephrine first; IV fluids; epinephrine infusion if refractory
NeurogenicWarm, dry skin; slow heart rate; cord injury T6 or aboveFluids, then norepinephrine; atropine for symptomatic bradycardia

Septic shock (Sepsis-3): vasopressors needed for MAP ≥65 mm Hg and lactate >2 mmol/L despite adequate fluids. SSC 2026 MAP goal: 65 (60–65 at age 65 or older).

Safety Typical adult doses for exam study; follow your institution's protocols. Anaphylaxis: epinephrine 0.01 mg/kg IM (1 mg/mL), mid-outer thigh; adults usually 0.3–0.5 mg (max 0.5 mg); repeat every 5–15 min if needed. Antihistamines and steroids never replace it.
Safety Fluids worsen LV cardiogenic shock with a high wedge and crackles. RV infarct is preload-dependent: cautious fluids, no nitrates or diuretics. Bleeding: limit crystalloid; accept SBP 80–90 until controlled, except with brain injury. Neurogenic: phenylephrine can worsen bradycardia.

SCAI SHOCK stages (cardiogenic)

StageMeaningTypical findings
A: At riskAMI or heart failure, perfusing wellWarm, normal lactate, SBP ≥100 (or baseline)
B: BeginningUnstable, still perfusingSBP <90, MAP <60 or >30 drop from baseline; HR ≥100; normal lactate
C: ClassicHypoperfused; needs a drug or deviceLactate ≥2, cold, urine <30 mL/h; CI <2.2, PCWP >15
D: DeterioratingFailing initial supportRising lactate; more pressors or MCS added
E: ExtremisCirculatory collapseCPR or near pulseless; lactate ≥8, pH <7.2

Cardiac arrest modifier: add only if comatose (GCS <9) after the arrest. Current US guidance: norepinephrine first; no routine IABP in MI-related shock.

Exam tip Exams test B versus C. Hypotension or tachycardia alone is stage B; any hypoperfusion (lactate ≥2, cold skin, low urine output) makes it C.

Cord injury MAP: a 2024 guideline suggests at least 75–80, not above 90–95, for 3–7 days. Many exam items still use 85–90 for 7 days.

Reading the numbers on exams

  1. Low SVR: distributive. Slow HR suggests neurogenic; hives or wheeze, anaphylaxis; infection, sepsis.
  2. High SVR, low CO: low-flow; check the filling pressures.
  3. CVP and wedge low: hypovolemic. Wedge above about 15–18: cardiogenic.
  4. High CVP with a low or normal wedge: PE or RV infarct.
  5. CVP, PA diastolic and wedge high and nearly equal: tamponade, with Beck triad and pulsus paradoxus (inspiratory SBP fall >10 mm Hg).
Exam tip Normals: CI 2.5–4.0 L/min/m², SVR 800–1,200 dynes·s/cm⁵, wedge 8–12 mm Hg, CVP 2–8 mm Hg, SvO2 60–80%.
